# Website & LinkedIn Copy — Outline

**Reader:** a bootstrapped, non-technical founder who arrives via a warm referral. They aren't comparing options. They're trying to confirm you're safe to bet the business on.
**Job of the copy:** reassure and validate the referral, then get them to book a call.
**Voice:** a product-minded partner. Calm, direct, senior. No hype.
**Platforms:** the website and LinkedIn tell the same story. LinkedIn is the condensed version.

---

## 1. Headline

- One line that says who you help and what you do.
- Name the reader (founders building custom software) and the outcome (the right product, built well, in market).
- Avoid "full-stack developer," tech stack lists, and "AI-powered."
- Keep a version short enough for the LinkedIn headline.

## 2. Their situation

- Show you understand the stakes: the software _is_ the business, and if the build fails, the venture fails.
- Name the fear without dramatizing it. They can't judge technical quality, so hiring feels like a leap of faith.
- Acknowledge the cost of delay: every day without a product in market costs time and money.
- Keep it short, two or three sentences. The point is "I get it," not "be afraid."

## 3. Your approach

- The core idea is **build the right thing**. Quality matters, but deciding what to build matters more.
- Be **ruthless about essentials**: you cut the product down to what actually captures the market, so they launch sooner and spend less.
- You help shape the scope and requirements. You don't just take orders.
- **AI (one or two sentences):** AI lets you move at the speed of a small team. Writing code is now cheap, but knowing which code the business needs and making it hold up with real customers is the job. Don't list tools.

## 4. How it works

1. previous cycle recap, new cycle assessment. We look at the project board and decide what is next
2. Tickets get fleshed out into implementation plans
3. Implementation, capture new tickets for future cycles if needed
4. Release and review in staging, revise if needed

- Discovery → roadmap → planning & design → development cycles & releases.
- Frame each step as a way of reducing risk, not as a process diagram.
- Show visibility: regular demos and one-on-one calls, so they always know where things stand.
- Address budget fears: how scope and cost stay predictable. _(Open: see item 8.)_
- If you adopt it, mention a fixed-price discovery phase as a low-risk first step. _(Open: see item 27.)_

## 5. What happens after launch

- **Core message:** they'll have the technical support their product needs as their business evolves.
- **After launch:** explain how support continues once customers start using the product. Launch isn't necessarily the end of the engagement.
- **As needs change:** the working relationship can range from occasional maintenance and fixes to continued product development, depending on what the product and business need.
- **As the team grows:** explain how you can help bring other developers into the work when appropriate, with continuity as responsibilities change.
- Emphasize a dependable technical partner and a working relationship that can evolve. Help the founder picture what comes next without implying they need to manage the technology themselves.
- Ownership of code, IP, and accounts, and freedom to choose who provides support, are supporting reassurances. Mention briefly if useful; they don't need to carry the section.
- Keep commitments grounded in the support and transition arrangements you actually offer.

## 6. Proof

- **Acquisition:** a product you built that was sold to a large company. This is your strongest proof point, so lead with it. _(Open: what can be named? See item 23.)_
- **Longevity:** Tap Bio, which ran from 2017 to 2026. Nine years shows you build things that last. _(Open: your role and what it was. See item 24.)_
- Two or three more outcomes, written as short "situation → result" lines. _(Open: see item 25.)_
- **Testimonials:** collect two or three short quotes from past clients. For a reader who can't judge code, this is the highest-impact addition. _(Open: see item 26.)_

## 7. Fit

- **For:** founders with a clear vision, real commitment, and a serious budget, who value quality, speed, and trust over the lowest price.
- **Not for:** ad-driven business models, or projects without a real budget.
- **Budget signal:** "Engagements typically start around $50k." This filters out poor fits and signals seniority.
- Stating who you're _not_ for reads as confidence, not exclusion.

## 8. Call to action

- **Book a call.** Make it the single, clear next step.
- Lower the stakes: a conversation about what they're building, not a sales pitch.
- Consider a line acknowledging the referral, e.g. "If someone sent you my way…"

## Open items

- **8:** what concretely keeps budgets from blowing up (per-phase estimates, fixed-price discovery, change handling)
- **20:** brand as yourself by name, or under a studio name
- **23:** can the acquired product and the acquirer be named?
- **24:** what Tap Bio was and your role in it
- **25:** two or three other projects worth featuring, with outcomes
- **26:** reach out to past clients for testimonials
- **27:** pricing model (fixed discovery, then phases or a retainer?)

**Excluded by choice:** location/time zone, and client capacity.
